Emissionsgate

A fictitious AutoCorp benchmarked against Volkswagen Group's real FY2022 Annual Report — what compliance actually costs, and what fixing it actually looks like.

redline

Everything under the Diagnostic and Retrofit stages below is drawn from Volkswagen Group's real, published 2022 Annual Report (VW+Annual+Report+2022.pdf) — it is the closest real-world analogue to the case, since Volkswagen's own 2015 "Dieselgate" emissions-cheating scandal is the evident real-world template the fictitious case is modelled on. 01

Ignition — the pressure that starts every cover-up

The case: AutoCorp (fictitious)

AutoCorp built its identity on performance and design leadership. When emissions regulation tightened globally, the company faced a real engineering constraint: hitting compliance limits without giving up the power and responsiveness its brand was sold on. That constraint was solvable through legitimate paths — smaller-displacement turbo engines, hybridization, longer development cycles. AutoCorp's leadership chose the faster path instead: adjust what the test measures, not what the car does.

This is the pattern worth naming, because it is not unique to cars. A real operating constraint meets a short-reporting-period incentive structure, and someone proposes a fix that satisfies the metric without satisfying the underlying requirement. The emissions test is the mechanism here; the same shape recurs in mis-sold financial products, falsified safety data, and inflated user-growth numbers elsewhere.

02

Misfire — where leadership actually failed

The failure at AutoCorp was not a single rogue engineer. The case describes closed-door executive discussions where the ethical dimension was raised, understood, and then set aside under pressure to protect quarterly targets and bonus thresholds. That is the leadership failure specifically: not ignorance of the ethics, but a decision process with no structural mechanism to let the ethical objection outrank the financial one.

The mechanism, stated plainly CEO Sarah Mitchell's team had the information needed to choose transparency. What they lacked was a governance structure — a compliance function with real authority, a reporting line independent of the business units it oversees — that could have stopped the decision before it reached the market. Ethical awareness without structural authority does not prevent misconduct; it just means the misconduct was informed.
03

Check engine — how concealment fails

AutoCorp's scheme ended the way most large-scale corporate concealment ends: an internal whistleblower, not an external auditor or regulator, made first contact. The company's first instinct — deny, and attribute the allegation to competitor sabotage — bought a few news cycles and then collapsed under mounting technical evidence. Denial followed by capitulation is close to the worst possible sequence: it forfeits the credibility a company needs most once it does eventually admit fault, and it signals to the market that leadership's first move under pressure is concealment, not correction.

04

Recall — the fallout, generalized

The case traces four channels of damage, and each has a real-world counterpart worth naming precisely, because "reputational damage" is usually where student analyses stop being specific:

ChannelWhat actually breaks
FinancialMarket value, share price, and — as the Volkswagen figures below show — a multi-year cash drain from fines, recalls, and litigation that outlasts the news cycle by a decade.
TalentEngineers and executives who did not sign up to be associated with a fraud case leave voluntarily, hollowing out exactly the technical capability needed to rebuild.
CompetitiveRivals reposition around the gap you just opened — "ethical," "transparent," "compliant" become their marketing, at your expense.
RegulatoryOversight tightens for the whole industry, not just the offending firm — the cost of one company's shortcut is paid by every competitor's compliance budget.

Diagnostic — what this actually cost, in real numbers

Volkswagen Group, FY2022 Annual Report — seven years after Dieselgate became public

Volkswagen's 2015 diesel-emissions scandal is the obvious real-world template behind this case. What its 2022 annual report shows is the part case studies rarely get to illustrate with real figures: the cost of concealment does not end when the story leaves the headlines. It becomes a recurring line item.

Read together, these numbers say something more precise than "scandals are expensive." They say the bill does not close. A one-time decision to cheat an emissions test in the 2000s was still generating disclosed legal risk, cash outflow, and a dedicated report line in a 2022 annual report — a cost horizon of well over a decade, against whatever near-term margin the original shortcut protected.

06

Retrofit — what leadership actually rebuilt

Not PR — structural change, evidenced in the same annual report

The assignment asks for proposed leadership activities to avoid a repeat. Rather than propose these in the abstract, here is what Volkswagen's real governance structure looked like before 2015 and what it discloses now — because the difference is the actual answer to the assignment question.

Structural gap, pre-2015
Real fix Volkswagen made
Evidence in the FY2022 report
No board-level owner for compliance — legal and ethics sat inside functions with production and sales targets.
Created a standalone Board of Management function: Integrity and Legal Affairs, reporting at the same level as Finance and Technology.
Function held by Hiltrud Dorothea Werner (2017–Jan 2022), then Dr. Manfred Düss (from Feb 2022) — a named, permanent Board seat.
Compliance activity was fragmented across HR, legal, and risk teams with no single accountable program.
Launched Together4Integrity (T4I) — eleven key initiatives unifying integrity, culture, compliance, risk and HR under one Group-wide program.
Named "a central pillar of the NEW AUTO Group strategy," with a public target of full rollout by 2025 and standing reports to the Board.
No safe, protected channel for an employee to report suspected wrongdoing internally before it became a regulator's problem.
Built a Group-wide whistleblower system as the central point of contact for reporting serious rule-breaking.
Described in the 2022 report as core infrastructure of the compliance management system, not an ad-hoc hotline.
Reputational and ESG standing had no formal recovery path — trust was assumed, not measured.
Completed an independent Monitorship and pursued reinstatement to the UN Global Compact as an explicit, trackable milestone.
Report states ESG ratings were "downgraded significantly" post-scandal and improved specifically after Monitorship completion.
The proposal, for AutoCorp Sarah Mitchell's team needs exactly this shape of fix, not a communications plan: a compliance officer with Board-level standing and a reporting line independent of the units under review; a named, public, multi-year integrity program with milestones the market can check; a whistleblower channel that existed before the crisis, not one built in response to it; and remuneration structures that price in long-horizon legal and reputational risk, not just this quarter's volume and margin.
07

Reflection & discussion

  • FeasibilityWould an "ethical oversight committee" have real teeth at AutoCorp, or does it become a second layer of process that gets worked around under enough pressure — the same way the original emissions decision was?
  • Power dynamicsVolkswagen's Integrity and Legal Affairs function reports at Board level specifically because a compliance officer without Board standing cannot override a business-unit head under quarterly pressure. Does AutoCorp's proposed committee have that standing, or is it advisory only?
  • Incentives vs. innovationRebalancing incentive metrics away from short-term volume risks slowing product cycles. Volkswagen's answer was to redirect the freed capital into R&D (€25.1bn, +21.3%) rather than cut spend — is that a realistic trade for AutoCorp, or does it require the cash cushion only a company AutoCorp's size may not have?
  • Trust is not linearVolkswagen's own disclosures show diesel-related legal costs were still rising in 2022, seven years on. What would actually convince a skeptical customer or regulator that AutoCorp's reforms are real rather than cosmetic — and how long should that take?

Case study: the "Emissionsgate" scandal, and what actually fixes it. The fictitious case (AutoCorp) follows a familiar shape: a real engineering constraint, a leadership team that discussed the ethics and set them aside under quarterly pressure, a whistleblower who forced the truth out, and a company that denied first and admitted later — the worst possible order. To ground the "how do you prevent this" question in something real, I benchmarked it against Volkswagen's actual 2022 Annual Report — the real-world case this one is clearly modelled on. Two things stood out: The bill never closes. VW is still booking diesel-issue costs as a named line item seven years after 2015 — a €10.6bn contingent liability, and cash outflows that were still rising in 2022, not falling. The real fix was structural, not cosmetic. VW created a standalone Board-level "Integrity and Legal Affairs" function, launched a named multi-year compliance program (Together4Integrity, 11 initiatives, public 2025 rollout target), and built a whistleblower channel as core infrastructure — not a PR response. The lesson for any leadership team: ethical awareness without structural authority doesn't prevent misconduct, it just means the misconduct was informed.
