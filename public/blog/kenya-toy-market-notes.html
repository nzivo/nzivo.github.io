Kenya Toy Market Notes

The same field research repeated for the Kenyan market: Jumia, Toyzoona, and Cheza Plus locally against Toys"R"Us, Hamleys, and LEGO globally — plus a 60-year-old Nairobi chain that shows this market is at an earlier stage of the same shift.

6 online stores · 3 KE local + 3 global Physical: Toyworld Kenya · desk research Companion to the Portugal field notes
01

Scope and method

How this was done — this repeats the same field-research method used for the Portugal version, swapping in three Kenyan online retailers as the "local" set. All 6 online storefronts were reviewed directly (live homepage/category review, Aug 2026). The physical chain, Toyworld Kenya, was not visited in person — it's desk-researched from the retailer's own published branch and business information, and is labeled as such.

Online stores reviewed — Local (Kenya): Jumia Kenya (Toys & Games), Toyzoona, Cheza Plus. Global: Toys"R"Us, Hamleys, LEGO — same three global benchmarks used in the Portugal comparison, to keep the two field notes directly comparable.

02

Three different toy businesses, all called "online toy store"

Where the Portuguese retailers converged on the same licensing mix with different loyalty mechanics, Kenya's three stores turned out to be genuinely different business models sitting side by side — price-led marketplace, franchise-led specialist, and franchise-free premium-educational.

The Kenyan market shows a segmentation the Portuguese one didn't as clearly: Jumia competes purely on price and delivery logistics as one category among thousands on a general marketplace; Toyzoona competes as a toy specialist on the same licensing playbook as the global chains; Cheza Plus opts out of franchise licensing entirely and sells trust in materials and pedagogy instead. Three different answers to the same market, coexisting.

03

Physical retail: reach first, theatre later (desk research, not visited)

Toyworld Kenya — 60 years on distribution, not activation

Toyworld has operated in Kenya for over 60 years and now runs six branches inside the country's leading malls — ABC Place, Yaya Centre, Village Market, Sarit Centre, Garden City in Nairobi, and City Mall in Mombasa. Its Village Market location, in particular, is positioned toward expat and upper-income families shopping for imported and educational brands.

Set against Hamleys' slime-making activations or LEGO's in-store AR "Digital Box," Toyworld's competitive advantage reads differently: it's built on being physically present in every major mall a target family already shops in, not on turning any single store into a destination experience. That's not a lesser strategy — it's the earlier, distribution-first stage of the same evolution Hamleys and LEGO have already moved past in more saturated, higher-rent retail markets.

04

The AR/digital-toy gap is the real finding

Globally, the digital shift shows up as LEGO Hidden Side's ghost-hunting AR app, Technic's app-unlocked racetracks, Nexo Knights' scannable shields, in-store AR preview kiosks, and Mattel's chip-embedded Hot Wheels id cars. None of it appeared on any of the three Kenyan storefronts reviewed.

That's worth reading as a market gap rather than a market failure. Toyzoona already carries the global franchise catalog (LEGO, Barbie, Marvel, Pokémon) at Kenyan price points — the AR/app layer that accompanies those same product lines elsewhere simply hasn't been positioned, priced, or marketed into this market yet. Whichever local distributor moves first on bringing an AR-connected line to market gets to define the category rather than compete inside an existing one.

Bottom line — Kenya's toy market isn't behind the digital shift so much as it's one stage earlier in it: still consolidating physical distribution and price competition, with the franchise layer already in place and the AR/digital layer still open for whoever claims it first.
