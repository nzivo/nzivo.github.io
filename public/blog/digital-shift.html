Digital Shift: the Toy Industry

Where AR/VR and hybrid play break the old marketing playbook, why "add digital features" is a riskier bet than it looks, and what the toy industry's real EV moment is turning out to be.

01

The marketing paradigm has flipped direction

The old model sold a single object once: shelf placement, a TV spot, a Christmas catalog. The new model has to sell — and keep selling — an ecosystem: a physical object, an app, an online community, and a stream of content that never stops after the purchase.

That reversal is the real paradigm break: brands used to launch a toy and hope a cartoon or ad drove kids to it. Now the digital narrative (a Fortnite island, a Minecraft build, a YouTube creator) comes first, and the physical product has to earn its place as an extension of an experience kids already know — which means marketing has shifted from campaign-based to always-on content operations, and cross-platform compatibility (iOS, Android, console, and whatever the app store approves this quarter) has become a marketing constraint, not just an engineering one.

02

The real risk isn't adding AR/VR — it's whose platform you depend on

The scenario frames this as a binary: modernize with AR/VR and risk pushing kids toward fully digital play, or stay physical and risk looking dated. The FY2025–26 numbers from Hasbro and Mattel suggest the binary is wrong — digital isn't the threat itself, dependency on someone else's digital ecosystem is.

Failure mode A — stay purely physical

Toys R Us didn't lose to AR/VR — it lost because it outsourced its own e-commerce to Amazon in the early 2000s and never rebuilt digital retail capability in-house. By the time it needed to compete on its own digital terms, the debt from its 2005 leveraged buyout left no room to catch up. It filed for bankruptcy in 2017 and liquidated its US stores in 2018.

Failure mode B — digitize into someone else's house

A toy that only functions as a login screen inside Fortnite, Roblox, or Minecraft builds engagement for the platform, not necessarily brand equity for the toy. If the digital layer isn't owned, the brand is one algorithm change or licensing renegotiation away from losing the relationship it just spent years building with that child.

Hasbro's numbers make the third option visible: Wizards of the Coast isn't an app bolted onto old toys, it's an owned digital-games business (Magic: The Gathering, Dungeons & Dragons digital tools) that competes directly with the video-game industry on its own turf, at software margins. That's a materially different bet than adding AR to a dinosaur toy.

03

Is this really the toy industry's EV moment?

Partly — but the sharper parallel isn't the petrol-to-electric transition; it's the retailer that never made any transition at all.

The EV analogy implies every player eventually gets there, just on different timelines, the way most automakers now field electric line-ups. Toys R Us is the more useful warning: it shows that "adapt eventually" isn't guaranteed to be survivable. A five-to-ten-year lag on digital capability didn't cost it market share — it ended the company.

  1. Don't treat "digitize every toy" as the goal.Pandemic-era data shows real, durable demand for screen-free, tactile, STEM, and sustainability-led play — a segment that AR/VR features don't serve and shouldn't be forced onto.
  2. Build (or buy) owned digital-game capability, not app add-ons.Hasbro's margin data is the clearest evidence in this analysis: an owned games studio competes at software economics; a companion app to a physical toy mostly doesn't.
  3. Run a barbell portfolio, not a single bet.Keep tactile/physical lines strong for the parents and kids actively choosing less screen time, while building genuinely competitive digital-game IP for the segment already living inside Fortnite, Roblox, and Minecraft — and measure each half on its own economics.
  4. Treat platform dependency as a strategic risk to manage, not a marketing channel to chase.Collaborations like LEGO Fortnite are valuable for reach, but should be paired with investment in owned digital properties the brand fully controls — the same way an automaker uses a battery-supplier partnership to get to market faster while still building its own EV platform.
Bottom line — the toy industry doesn't need every product to go digital. It needs a clear answer to one question per line: does this experience live on a platform we own, or one we're a guest on — and can the business model survive either answer if the guest arrangement ends?
