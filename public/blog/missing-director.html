NR! Module · Problem-Solving Scenario

The Missing Director

A key-person risk reading of the manufacturing company that centralized every decision in one CEO — where the real losses show up, and the protections that go beyond the scenario's own fixes.

Scenario: The Missing Director (fictional) Lens: key-person risk & governance
01

What actually broke

The scenario reads as a burnout story, but the burnout is a symptom. The structural failure happened earlier: the company built a single point of failure on purpose, by design, in the name of efficiency.

Centralizing decisions and stripping middle managers of authority didn't just make the CEO overloaded — it made every process in the company dependent on that one person staying present, informed, and well. The moment he left, the company didn't lose a manager; it lost its entire decision-making capacity at once, because nothing had been built to route around him.

Suppliers→ Projects→ CEO (sole authority)← Middle managers← Owners
"Middle managers are caught in a paradox — their authority was stripped by the previous CEO, leaving them ill-equipped to make crucial decisions in his absence." — The Missing Director, Act 3
02

Where the loss actually lands

"The company struggled" understates it. Six distinct forms of loss compound on top of each other in the weeks after a sole decision-maker disappears — and most of them outlast the month the case describes.

TypeWhere it shows upWhy it compounds
Operational Stalled projects, confused suppliers, unapproved purchase orders No one holds sign-off authority, so work doesn't fail loudly — it just quietly stops.
Financial & legal Bank mandates, loan covenants, and contracts tied to a named signatory "Key person" clauses in credit agreements can trigger covenant review or default risk — this is rarely just an inconvenience.
Human capital Demotivated, disempowered middle managers The people the company most needs to step up were trained, for a year, not to.
Tacit knowledge Client history, informal supplier terms, undocumented decisions If it only ever lived in one person's head, it leaves the building with them.
Reputational Supplier and customer confidence, owner credibility with lenders/investors External parties read internal chaos as a signal about the whole company, not just one role.
Strategic Missed market windows, delayed decisions, competitor advantage Paralysis is invisible on a balance sheet until a competitor fills the gap.
03

Protecting against it

The scenario's own seven fixes — distributed decision rights, cross-training, documentation, succession planning, agile principles, technology, and a culture of ownership — are the right foundation. Two protections worth adding sit above that foundation, at the governance level.

A documented Delegation of Authority matrix

Verbal delegation isn't delegation the bank, a court, or a supplier will recognize under pressure. A DoA matrix names, in writing, who can approve what spend, sign what contract, and act as alternate signatory — filed with legal counsel and the company's bank before it's needed, not improvised on crisis day.

Treating burnout as a governance risk

The scenario frames the CEO's exit as a personal breakdown. Structurally, it's the predictable output of a system with no upper bound on one role's control. Protecting the company from over-reliance on one leader also means protecting that leader from carrying unlimited authority — workload and decision-load audits belong at board level, not just HR.

  1. Distribute decision rights by design, not just after a crisis.Middle managers keep real authority within their domain from day one — centralization should be the exception that gets escalated, not the default operating mode.
  2. Fund key-person insurance for irreplaceable roles.addedA financial buffer that covers the cost of an emergency search, interim leadership, and lost momentum while the succession plan actually activates.
  3. Pre-agree a crisis playbook, not just a succession list.Naming a potential successor isn't the same as defining what happens on day one of an unplanned absence: who convenes, who signs, who communicates to suppliers and clients within 48 hours.
  4. Keep a living knowledge base, not a person's inbox.SOPs and documentation only protect the company if they're centrally stored, version-controlled, and reviewed — not scattered across one executive's email and memory.
  5. Give the board a cadence independent of the CEO.addedRegular owner/board reviews of strategic decisions mean the company's direction was never solely a function of one person's judgment in the first place.
Bottom line — resilience isn't the absence of a strong leader; it's a structure engineered to survive losing one — financially, legally, operationally, and culturally — without the loss ever becoming existential.
