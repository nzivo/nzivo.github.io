Ethical Dilemmas in Branding

The tightrope between popularity and responsibility, tested against a real company's own compliance failures and five current controversies across alcohol, sugar drinks, and gambling.

Scenario: Ethical Dilemmas (fictional narratives) Grounded in PMI's real 2019 marketing audit +5 current cases, 2025–2026
01

The tightrope, in one line

Sarah (tobacco), Michael (e-cigarettes), Emily (alcohol), and David (sugar drinks) all face the same structural problem: the marketing techniques that build the strongest brands — emotional resonance, aspirational imagery, social proof, youth-adjacent culture — are exactly the techniques most likely to reach and influence the people regulation exists to protect.

That's not a coincidence the article's four case studies happen to share. It's the actual mechanism. Brand equity is built through association, not information — and association doesn't respect an age gate the way a warning label does.

02

Why "ethical branding" frameworks don't fully apply here

The Branding Journal's ethical branding guide proposes eight pillars a brand should satisfy: purpose, values, products/services, production, supply chain, communication, stakeholders, and community contribution. Most of these are achievable for a tobacco, alcohol, or sugar-drink company. One structurally isn't.

A company can genuinely excel at pillars 1, 2, 4–8 — and PMI's own report is a good example of taking pillar 6 (communication) seriously. But pillar 3 is definitionally out of reach for a combustible tobacco product. What's actually available to these industries isn't "ethical branding" in the full sense of the framework — it's ethical marketing practice: honest, restrained, age-gated communication around a product that can't itself clear the bar. That's a real and valuable thing to build. It's just a narrower claim than the framework implies, and worth naming precisely rather than blurring the two.

03

What rigorous self-regulation actually looks like — and where it still leaks

PMI's 2019 "Responsible Marketing Practices" report is unusual: a company voluntarily publishing its own audit, including the parts where its own standards were breached. That transparency is itself a data point on the ethical-branding question — and the failures it discloses are more informative than the successes.

The system, at scale

Four non-negotiable principles (market only to adult smokers, warn of health effects, be honest, follow the law), a ban on social media marketing for combustibles since 2015, and Good Conversion Practices restricting smoke-free products to existing adult users only. 86.9% of employees trained on the code in 2018; over 30,000 people trained on smoke-free sales rules in 2019 alone.

Where it still broke

An Indonesian mobile app collected consumers' declared age but never verified it — a real gap despite the policy requiring verification. Paid digital influencers in Russia and Europe turned out to be legal-age smokers who were nonetheless under PMI's own 25-year-old minimum for anyone appearing in its marketing.

"While there were a few instances when we fell short... our monitoring and compliance systems are working as intended." — PMI, Responsible Marketing Practices at PMI, December 2019

The point isn't that PMI is uniquely lax — if anything, this is one of the more rigorous, publicly documented compliance systems in the industry, built in response to a shareholder resolution from Trinity Health. The point is that even this system leaked precisely at the edges regulation struggles to reach: third-party platforms, new digital formats, influencer marketing. That's the pattern worth watching for in every industry below.

04

Five current cases, three more markets

AlcoholMay 2025

The UK Advertising Standards Authority reprimanded FourLoko for a social media ad promoting irresponsible drinking, breaching the CAP Code's requirement that alcohol marketing be socially responsible.

AlcoholMarch 2025

The ASA separately reprimanded Bargain Booze for an ad found to have particular appeal to under-18s — a distinct code breach from irresponsible-drinking messaging, showing the two failure modes (encouraging excess vs. reaching minors) recur independently.

Alcohol — zero-alcohol "shadow marketing"Australian research, 2023–24

Researchers found alcohol brands using zero-alcohol variants to advertise in settings the alcoholic product legally can't touch — nearly two-thirds of campaigns studied showed the zero-alcohol product used in traditionally alcohol-free contexts like swimming or driving. Because branding is shared with the parent alcoholic product, this builds brand affinity in unregulated territory: not a violation, a genuine gap in how the rules were written.

Sugar drinksOngoing, US

Sugary-drink advertising in the US is disproportionately targeted at Black and Hispanic youth, and parents describe front-of-package claims on children's sugar-sweetened drinks as actively deceptive about health benefits — the same "reaches the vulnerable audience the ad wasn't nominally for" pattern seen in tobacco and alcohol, without any addictive-chemical component to explain it.

Gambling — the newest frontier2025

Sports-betting integration into live broadcasts has coincided with real integrity scandals — an NBA player pleading guilty to conspiracy for betting against his own team, another arrested in a federal gambling probe — and a measurable trust collapse: 43% of Americans now call legal sports betting bad for society, up from 34% in 2022, with 63% supporting a federal ban on in-game sportsbook ads.

05

A working framework for the marketer actually facing this

  1. Separate "ethical branding" from "ethical marketing practice."If the product itself can't clear an honest harm bar, don't claim the brand is ethical — claim, specifically and only, that the practice around it (honesty, age-gating, restraint) is. Overclaiming here is itself the deception the frameworks warn against.
  2. Assume the leak is at the edge, not the center.PMI's own failures weren't in its core cigarette advertising — they were in a new app and a new influencer channel. Any responsible-marketing programme should treat every new platform or format as a compliance gap until proven otherwise, not as a growth channel to exploit before rules catch up.
  3. Watch for "adjacent product" loopholes.Zero-alcohol shadow marketing shows how a genuinely lower-harm product variant can be used to route brand-building through unregulated space. The same pattern is worth watching for wherever a "safer" line sits next to a regulated one — reduced-sugar, low-nicotine, "responsible gambling" tools — the adjacent product can carry the parent brand's equity past the rules built for the original.
  4. Treat "responsible use" campaigns as necessary but insufficient.Anti-drunk-driving and smoking-cessation campaigns are genuinely good — and they don't resolve the core tension, because they run alongside, not instead of, marketing built on the same emotional mechanism the campaign is trying to counteract.
Bottom line — the real ethical dilemma in these markets was never "should we market the product." It's that the only tool available for building a strong brand — emotional association — is the same tool regulation exists to keep away from the people it's meant to protect. No compliance system, however rigorous, fully closes that gap; the honest position is to say so, not to claim the gap has been closed.
