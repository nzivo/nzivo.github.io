Toy Store Field Notes

Promotion, bundling, and franchise co-marketing across six online toy retailers and three physical chains — held up against a 1990s toy shelf.

6 online stores · 3 PT local + 3 global 3 physical chains · desk research Baseline: 1990s childhood
01

Scope and method

How this was done — the 6 online stores below were reviewed directly (live homepage review, Aug 2026): 3 Portuguese retailers and 3 global chains, matching the assignment's local/global split. The 3 physical chains were not visited in person — they're desk-researched from current retailer press coverage and each chain's own published in-store programs, and are labeled as such throughout. The "childhood" column reflects general, widely-shared 1990s toy-marketing touchstones rather than one specific person's memory, and is written to be personalized before posting.

Online stores reviewed — Local (Portugal): Drim.pt, Brincatoys.pt, Magia do Brinquedo. Global: Toys"R"Us (toysrus.com), Hamleys (hamleys.com), LEGO (lego.com/pt-pt and lego.com AR programs).

02

What the 6 storefronts are actually selling

Every store, local or global, leads with the same three levers: percentage-off flash sales, franchise licensing stacked across multiple properties at once, and a loyalty or omnichannel hook — not the toy's own features.

The pattern that stands out: no store, local or global, sells a single franchise anymore. Every catalog runs 4–6 licensed universes in parallel, and the local Portuguese retailers mirror the global players' licensing mix almost exactly — the difference between them is loyalty mechanics and delivery speed, not what's on the shelf.

03

Physical retail: from shelf-space to experience (desk research, not visited)

Three chains illustrate the same shift from a different angle: the physical store's job has moved from "hold inventory" to "manufacture an experience e-commerce can't replicate."

Hamleys — theatre as retail

The Regent Street flagship runs a "Gootopia" slime-making activation, in-store performers, meet-and-greets, and even a branded sightseeing bus tour through London. The toys are almost secondary to the visit itself.

LEGO — the AR fitting room

In-store "Digital Box" kiosks let a shopper hold a physical set box up to a screen and watch a full 3D animated build appear before they buy — augmented reality used as a try-before-you-buy tool, not just a post-purchase feature.

Toys"R"Us — the shop-in-shop pivot

Rather than rebuild standalone stores after its 2018 collapse, the brand relaunched as 450+ branded shop-in-shops inside Macy's locations across the US, alongside a handful of destination flagships (American Dream, Mall of America). It re-entered physical retail by embedding inside someone else's foot traffic, not by competing for its own square footage.

04

AR and digital toys observed

  • LEGO Hidden Side — physical haunted-house sets paired with a ghost-hunting AR app that overlays hidden characters and puzzles onto the real model.
  • LEGO Technic AR — pull-back Technic vehicles unlock AR racetracks and steering challenges through a companion app.
  • LEGO Nexo Knights — scannable in-box shields upgrade a companion game's in-app powers, linking the physical purchase directly to digital progression.
  • LEGO in-store Digital Box — AR retail kiosk turning any shelf box into a rotatable 3D preview before purchase.
  • Mattel Hot Wheels id — physical die-cast cars with embedded chips that scan into a racing app to unlock stats, ghost-racing, and digital challenges.
  • Merge Cube — a palm-sized physical cube that acts as a marker-based AR display/controller, sold through Walmart and online, used across multiple third-party game titles for younger players.
05

Then vs. now — a 1990s toy shelf vs. today's

Growing up in the 1990s, the toys that mattered were things like a Game Boy, a shelf of Beanie Babies, LEGO Space or Ninja Turtles sets, and Saturday-morning cartoon tie-in figures. (Swap in your own specific toys/brands here before posting — this frame is built to be personalized.)

1990s
  • Discovery — Saturday-morning cartoons, TV ads, and the printed holiday catalog "wish book," circled in pencil.
  • Purchase — one transaction, one object; nothing to install, unlock, or update afterward.
  • Franchise tie-in — a single license per toy line (Star Wars figures, Ninja Turtles vehicles), tied to a movie's theatrical run.
  • Scarcity — playground word-of-mouth and real shelf shortages (Beanie Babies, Tickle Me Elmo).
  • Physical store — the destination itself; browsing the aisle was the entertainment.
Today
  • Discovery — YouTube unboxings, TikTok, and in-game crossovers (LEGO inside Fortnite) reach kids before a store ever does.
  • Purchase — the box is step one; an app, loyalty account, or AR layer continues the experience indefinitely.
  • Franchise tie-in — 4–6 licensed universes running in every catalog simultaneously, independent of any single release date.
  • Scarcity — engineered flash discounts (60–70% sales) and loyalty-tier cashback replace organic shortage.
  • Physical store — a branded activation (slime labs, AR kiosks) or a shop-in-shop riding someone else's foot traffic, not a standalone destination.
